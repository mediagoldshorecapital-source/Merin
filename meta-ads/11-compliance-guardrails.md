# 11 — Compliance Guardrails: Read This Before You Write Anything

Supplement advertising on Meta gets rejected for reasons that have nothing to do with whether the claim is true. Most beginners lose their first two weeks to rejections and, worse, some get their ad account restricted before they've learned anything.

This file is the practical version. It is **not legal advice** — have a qualified attorney and your regulatory consultant review your claim set and your substantiation file before you spend at scale.

---

## The three rulebooks you're operating under

| Rulebook | Who enforces | What it kills |
|---|---|---|
| **Meta Advertising Standards** | Meta's automated + human review | Your ad, then your ad account |
| **FTC Act §5 + Health Products Compliance Guidance** | Federal Trade Commission | Your company, via monetary judgments |
| **DSHEA / FDA rules on dietary supplements** | Food & Drug Administration | Warning letters, seizure, injunction |

Meta rejection is the annoyance. FTC and FDA are the real risk.

---

## Guardrail 1 — Meta's Personal Attributes policy (the #1 cause of rejection)

Meta prohibits ads that **assert or imply knowledge of a person's personal characteristics** — including health condition, physical condition, age, and medical status.

This means the direct second-person phrasing that feels most like good copywriting is exactly what gets rejected.

### The rewrite table — memorise this pattern

| ❌ Rejected | ✅ Approved | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Feeling more tired as you age?" | "Most women over 60 say they wake up tired after a full night's sleep." | Removes the assertion about *this viewer's* age and health |
| "Your brain fog isn't normal." | "I thought my brain fog was normal. It wasn't." | First-person narrative about the speaker |
| "You're 62 and exhausted." | "I'm 62. I was exhausted." | Same fact, speaker's own attribute |
| "Struggling with menopause fatigue?" | "Post-menopause fatigue is one of the most common complaints we hear." | Third-person, general |
| "Stop letting YOUR energy crash ruin your afternoons." | "The afternoon crash doesn't have to be the end of the day." | Removes possession of the condition |
| "At your age, NAD+ has already dropped 50%." | "After 50, NAD+ levels drop significantly." | General statement, not about the viewer |

### The three safe framings — use these and you'll almost never be rejected

1. **First-person narrative** — "I was…", "I noticed…", "I stopped…" (the speaker owns the attribute)
2. **Third-person / statistical** — "Most women over 60…", "Research shows that after 50…"
3. **General statements about the world** — "The afternoon crash is real."

> **Every hook in `04-hook-library.md` is already written in one of these three framings.** If you write your own, run it through the table above before filming. Rewriting after the shoot is expensive.

### Also prohibited by Meta for this category

- **Before/after images** showing bodily transformation, and images implying unrealistic outcomes. *(This is why static layout S-7 is a before/after of a **day**, not a body.)*
- **Zoomed-in body parts** — close-ups of a torso, sagging skin, etc.
- **Negative self-perception** — images or copy designed to make someone feel bad about their body
- **Unsafe or prohibited supplements** — Meta maintains a list; check it against your formula
- **Misleading health claims** and "miracle cure" framing
- Supplement ads may be **age-restricted to 18+** automatically

## Guardrail 2 — Disease claims (the FDA line)

This is the bright line in supplement advertising and it's simpler than people think.

> **You may describe an effect on the structure or function of the body. You may not claim to diagnose, treat, cure, mitigate, or prevent a disease.**

Crossing that line makes the product an unapproved drug in the FDA's eyes — regardless of whether the claim is true.

| ❌ Disease claim (never) | ✅ Structure/function (permitted with substantiation) |
|---|---|
| "Prevents Alzheimer's" / "reduces dementia risk" | "Supports cognitive function and mental clarity" |
| "Treats chronic fatigue syndrome" | "Supports healthy cellular energy production" |
| "Reverses ageing" | "Supports healthy ageing" |
| "Cures menopause symptoms" | "Supports energy levels during and after menopause" |
| "Fixes your metabolism" | "Supports normal metabolic function" |
| "Prevents cognitive decline" | "Supports memory and focus" |

### The brain-fog angle — handle with real care

Angle A4 and hooks H03 and H08 touch the most powerful fear in your dataset: *"Am I losing my mind?"* You can use it — but the ad must stay on the **speaker's private fear**, and must never connect the product to a disease outcome.

- ✅ "Every time I forget a word there's two seconds where I think, is this how it started for my mother?" *(first-person fear)* → *"…so I started taking something that supports mental clarity."*
- ❌ Anything that follows that fear with a claim that the product prevents, reduces the risk of, or protects against dementia or Alzheimer's.

The distinction is not cosmetic. The first is a woman describing her life. The second is an unapproved drug claim aimed at frightened people — and it is both illegal and genuinely harmful.

### The required disclaimer

On the landing page, and on the ad creative where claims appear:

> *These statements have not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. This product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.*

## Guardrail 3 — Substantiation (the FTC line)

The FTC standard is **competent and reliable scientific evidence** — for health claims, generally well-designed human clinical studies of the product or its key ingredients. "It's on our supplier's sell sheet" is not substantiation.

### Every number in your ads needs a file

Create a **claims substantiation folder** and put a source in it for each of these before it appears in a creative:

| Claim used in this SOP | What you need on file |
|---|---|
| "64% of NMN products fail lab tests" | The actual report/study, with date, methodology, and sample. Cite it visibly. |
| "NAD+ drops up to 50% after 50" | The peer-reviewed source. Note the population studied. |
| "1000mg pure NMN Complex" | Your own COA showing verified potency, per batch |
| "Third-party lab tested" | Current COA from a named independent lab, published or available on request |
| "Forbes / ELLE" | Screenshot + live URL of the actual mention. **A paid placement or a listicle mention is not an endorsement** — don't imply it is. |
| "5,128 reviews / 500K+ customers" | Live platform export, refreshed monthly |
| "90-day money-back guarantee" | Wording matching your published policy exactly, including who pays return shipping |
| "Most people notice around week three" | Must reflect **typical** experience, not your best testimonial |

### Testimonials — the rules that get brands sued

Under the FTC's endorsement guides:

1. **Real people, real experiences.** No fabricated, composite, or actor-portrayed testimonials presented as genuine customers.
2. **Results must be typical**, or the atypical result must be clearly disclosed. "Results may vary" in 8px grey at the bottom is not a disclosure — the FTC has said so repeatedly.
3. **Disclose material connections.** A paid creator must disclose it. `#ad` or "paid partnership" is the minimum; on Meta use the Paid Partnership tag.
4. **Experts must actually be experts.** A person in a white coat who is not a healthcare professional is a deceptive endorsement. This is why `07-production-sop.md` says never stage a fake white coat.
5. **Don't edit a testimonial into a different claim.** Trimming for length is fine; splicing "I felt better" onto a claim she never made is not.

### The honest-ad advantage

Script 5 says: *"It didn't help me sleep and it didn't change my weight."* Script 4's H26 hook is literally *"…and one thing that didn't change at all."*

This is deliberate. Stating a limitation is simultaneously your **best compliance posture** and your **best conversion device** with a buyer whose defining trait is that she has been burned before and is reading your ad looking for a reason not to buy. Honesty converts her. It isn't a tax on performance; with this specific customer it *is* the performance.

## Guardrail 4 — The NMN regulatory question (know this before you scale)

**NMN's status as a dietary supplement ingredient in the US has been actively disputed.** The FDA has taken the position that NMN is excluded from the dietary supplement definition on the grounds that it was authorised for investigation as a new drug — a position that has been contested by industry, subject to citizen petitions, and has already caused major retail platforms to delist NMN products.

**What this means for you, practically:**

- This is a **known business risk**, not a hypothetical one. Retailer delistings have happened before and could affect your channels.
- **Verify the current regulatory status with qualified regulatory counsel before scaling spend.** The position may have shifted since this SOP was written; do not rely on this paragraph as current.
- Keep your claim set conservative. A category under regulatory scrutiny is a category where aggressive claims attract attention fastest.
- Have a contingency: if a platform restricts the category, which of your assets and audiences transfer to another product?

This isn't a reason not to advertise. It's a reason to know the ground you're standing on.

## Guardrail 5 — Account safety hygiene

Losing the ad account is worse than losing a campaign. Protect it.

- [ ] **Business Manager verified**, domain verified, two-factor authentication on every admin
- [ ] **A backup ad account and a backup Business Manager exist** and are warm
- [ ] Payment method is stable; a failed payment can trigger a review
- [ ] You have not appealed rejections aggressively or repeatedly — repeated appeals on genuinely non-compliant ads are a risk signal
- [ ] Landing page matches the ad. **Meta reviews the destination too** — a compliant ad pointing at a page full of disease claims gets both flagged
- [ ] Comments moderated daily; you are not leaving user comments making medical claims on your behalf under a live ad
- [ ] All claims on the landing page pass the same tests as claims in the ad

### When an ad is rejected

1. **Read the specific policy cited.** Don't guess.
2. **Fix the actual issue** — usually a personal-attributes phrasing or an implied claim.
3. **Appeal once**, with a clear note explaining the change.
4. If rejected twice, **rebuild the creative** rather than appealing a third time.
5. Log every rejection and its cause in your tracker. After 20 launches you'll know your own patterns and stop hitting them.

## The pre-flight compliance check

Run this on every creative before it ships. It takes 90 seconds.

```
[ ] No sentence asserts what the VIEWER is, feels, or has
    (search the script for "you are", "your", "at your age", "struggling with")
[ ] No disease is named as prevented, treated, cured, or made less likely
[ ] Every number has a source in the substantiation folder
[ ] Every testimonial is real, unedited in substance, and typical
[ ] Paid creators are disclosed
[ ] Any expert on camera holds the credential shown
[ ] The FDA disclaimer appears where claims are made
[ ] No before/after of a body; no zoomed body parts; no shame framing
[ ] The landing page makes the same claims as the ad, and no bigger ones
[ ] I could defend every claim in this ad to a regulator with the file I have
```

That last line is the real test. If you couldn't produce the evidence on demand, don't run the ad.
