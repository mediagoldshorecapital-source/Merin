Part 01

Read the room before you write a word

This corpus is not a general population. It is the comment sections of NMN debate videos — Sinclair explainers, Thomas DeLauer sponsorships, Dr Brad Stanfield debunkings, third-party purity tests, FDA-ban news. That single fact should reset your creative brief.

These people are not unaware. They are adjudicating.

They arrive already fluent in NAD+, precursor, salvage pathway, TMG, sublingual, third-party tested. They quote study authors by name — Igarashi 2022, Yi 2022, Yoshino 2021. They compare cost-per-gram in public. Awareness-stage advertising ("Did you know your NAD+ declines with age?") is talking beneath them and will read as amateur.

You are writing for a product-aware, actively skeptical comparison shopper. The job of your ad is not to introduce the category. It is to win an argument they are already having.

The dominant emotion is not hope. It is betrayal.

Sinclair appears 247 times, and in the recent layers of the corpus the tone has flipped hard — from "his book is incredible" to "the more I learn about Sinclair the more I distrust him." Then the leading skeptic, Stanfield, got accused of his own product failing a Consumer Lab test. Both sides of the debate now stand accused of selling something.

"Dr Stanfield you have a shop too so why should we believe you when you have a financial interest too?"

Comment

The strategic consequence is severe and specific: your credibility cannot be borrowed from a scientist, a doctor, or an influencer. Every human authority in this category has been publicly compromised in front of this exact audience. What survives is documents — lab reports, batch numbers, named testing facilities, plain arithmetic.

Sampling caveat, stated plainly. Comment sections over-represent people with strong opinions and outcomes at both extremes. Treat the frequencies here as a map of what gets argued about, not as market-share estimates of belief. The verbatims are the asset; the counts are the ranking.

Part 03

Seven kinds of skeptic, seven different jobs

"Skepticism" is not one objection. It is seven distinct mental models, and a creative that answers one of them can actively inflame another. Treat these as separate audiences with separate ads.

The Reductionist

279 comments

"NR and NMN are extremely expensive sources of NA and NAM. Nothing more."

Belief
Gut bacteria break NMN down into plain niacin, so you're paying 15× for vitamin B3.
Don't
Argue enzymology in a 30-second ad. You cannot win the NAMPT-bottleneck debate in a feed, and trying makes you sound defensive.
Do
Move the argument to ground you can win: the flush. Many of them have tried high-dose niacin and hated it. "No flush, no 3-gram megadose, no rash" is a lived-experience claim they can verify tonight.
Asset
Side-by-side: what you swallow, what it feels like, what it costs per day.

The Counterfeit Cynic

167 comments

"Testing revealed the majority of NMN sold on Amazon is counterfeit or contains virtually no active ingredient."

Belief
The category is full of powder that isn't NMN. They cite the ChromaDex figures — 64% under 1% claimed content, 14% with zero — from memory.
Why it matters
This is the most winnable objection in the category and the least served. It is also the one Prime is structurally positioned to win.
Do
Make purity the hero, not longevity. Batch number on the bottle, scan to the actual COA, name the lab, show the assay number, publish failed batches too.
Asset
The COA is the creative. Not a badge — the document.

The Evidentialist

438 comments

"Ok but has any study on humans shown that higher NAD actually means people are better off?"

Belief
Mice aren't people. Blood NAD+ isn't tissue NAD+. Raising a biomarker isn't a benefit.
Do
Cite honestly and cite the limits. This buyer converts on calibration, not enthusiasm. A brand that says "here's what the 250 mg Igarashi trial showed, and here's what it didn't" out-credentials every competitor instantly.
Asset
A plain-language evidence page with a "what we can't claim yet" section. Long-form, not a feed ad.

The Conflict Detector

127 comments

"It's all grifters calling out grifters to sell their own grift."

Belief
Anyone who profits is disqualified from telling the truth. Sinclair, Stanfield, DeLauer, the sponsored reviewer — all compromised.
Do
Pre-empt it in your own voice. Say you sell it. Say what your margin pays for. Point them to a third party you don't control and invite them to check.
Never
Use a white coat, a stethoscope, or an unnamed "leading longevity researcher." This audience reads that as a tell.

The Disconfirmed Buyer

37 comments

"I've taken this for months and I feel nothing at all. It may work for some, but I will not be buying again."

Belief
I ran the experiment on myself. It failed. Your ad is contradicting my own body.
Do
Never tell them they're wrong. Give them a mechanism: dose too low, no methyl support, degraded product, already-sufficient NAD+, wrong window. Then give them the refund without a fight — the corpus shows a refunded customer still argues for the category.
Asset
"Five reasons NMN did nothing for you." This page does not exist anywhere. Build it.

The Placebo Self-Doubter

96 comments

"It's not placebo — I didn't notice the benefits until I stopped taking it."

Belief
I feel better, but I'm suspicious of myself. I need a way to prove it to me.
Insight
They invented their own test: stop taking it and see what comes back. It recurs constantly and it is the market's own gold standard of proof.
Do
Name the test and invite it. "Take it 60 days. Then stop for ten. That's the only review we care about." Confident, falsifiable, and completely honest.

The Contrarian

166 comments · tailwind

"When I heard the FDA wanted to ban it, I knew instantly it would be effective."

Note
The five highest-liked comments in the entire corpus are FDA-ban posts — 1,000, 615, 610, 586, 574 likes. This is the single strongest emotional current in the data.
Do
Use availability and supply framing: "still available," "we hold inventory," "stock up." Reactance sells here.
Careful
Don't make the conspiracy claim yourself. Let the sentiment exist in your audience; don't put "Big Pharma doesn't want you to have this" in your own ad copy. It's a regulatory and brand-safety liability, and it caps you at the fringe.

Part 04

The psychology underneath the objections

Below the questions sit six structural conditions of this category. These are the things that don't change when a new study drops — and therefore the things worth building a brand on.

The verification vacuum

NMN asks a buyer to accept three unverifiable things at once. They cannot feel it working (most report nothing dramatic). They cannot see it working (no visible change). And they cannot confirm the powder is even the molecule on the label. No other supplement category stacks all three.

Every anxiety in this corpus routes back to that vacuum — the purity panic, the placebo litigation, the refrigeration fear, the NAD blood tests, the pets used as unbiased test subjects. The brand that collapses the verification gap owns the category. Not the brand with the best story about longevity.

Authority collapse

This audience bought in on a Harvard professor and then watched him get accused of lobbying to turn their supplement into a prescription drug. They turned to the skeptic and watched his own multivitamin get accused of failing a lab test. The corpus records the whole arc.

The behavioural residue is a permanent discount applied to any confident human on camera. It's why "Thomas, you never actually said what happened to you" is one of the most-liked comment patterns in the set — 558 likes on one instance. Enthusiasm now reads as a sales tell. Specificity reads as truth.

Folk-science proof rituals

Denied trustworthy institutions, the market built its own evidence formats. Four recur constantly, and they are the native language of proof here:

  • The stop-and-restart test. "When I stop taking it, the arthritic pain comes back."
  • The physical benchmark. "I'm 57 and I can do more pushups than at bootcamp when I was 18." Mile times, stairs, pickleball, grip.
  • Before-and-after bloodwork. A1c, ApoB, plasma NAD+, biological-age tests.
  • The pet as control. "The dog can't have a placebo effect." Used sincerely and repeatedly.

Testimonials that use these formats land. Testimonials that say "I feel amazing" do not.

Age as an identity gate

"You're too young to need this" is used as a put-down in the comments — and simultaneously, being visibly old is the thing everyone is running from. The audience needs permission to claim decline without claiming oldness.

The language that works threads that needle by pointing at the biology, not the person: "NAD+ falls roughly 50% between 40 and 60" lets a 52-year-old opt in without accepting the label "elderly." The language that fails is "for seniors," "for mature adults," or stock photography of grey-haired couples on beaches.

Protocol anxiety after purchase

Refrigerate or not. Sublingual or swallow. With fat or empty stomach. TMG or not. Cycle or daily. Every one of these is an open loop that fires after the card is charged — which means it drives non-adherence, early churn, and refund requests, not just pre-purchase hesitation.

Treat protocol content as a retention lever with a hard revenue number attached, not as customer-service overhead.

Public cost-per-gram arithmetic

They do the math out loud, and they do it accurately: "$50 for 60 caps at 250 mg means 15 days at Sinclair's dose — $120 a month." "Sinclair's own brand is 15–20× the cost of third-party tested NMN by content weight."

Any brand that obscures serving math gets caught and mocked in its own comment section. The inverse is a real weapon: publish cost-per-gram and let the premium brands lose the comparison for you.

Part 06

Nine ideas worth building on

Ranked by the gap between how much the market wants it and how little anyone is supplying it.

1. Sell the certificate, not the molecule

Purity is the category's largest unresolved anxiety and its most concrete, provable claim. Batch number printed on every bottle, QR to the actual PDF, named lab, assay result, heavy metals, mycotoxins. Not a trust badge — the document itself, legible, dated, matching the batch in their hand. This converts the Counterfeit Cynic and reassures every other segment at the same time.

2. Reframe from "does NMN work" to "are you a responder"

The category's central argument is unwinnable and exhausting. The responder frame sidesteps it entirely, is more honest than the alternative, and is already how the smartest commenters talk. Operationalise it: a defined trial period, a specific protocol, a stated set of signals to watch for, and a no-argument refund if nothing shows up. You look confident because you're the only one willing to be tested.

3. Tell an honest timeline

The corpus is full of people quitting at three weeks because an ad implied two. The reported pattern clusters at 2–8 weeks, with sleep and morning alertness first, recovery and stamina later. A calibrated timeline — "most people notice nothing in week one; here's the order things tend to show up" — lowers refunds, raises adherence, and reads as truth precisely because it under-promises.

4. Own "why you felt nothing"

Zero brands have this page. It captures the highest-intent skeptical search traffic in the category, converts the Disconfirmed Buyer who is currently lost forever, and signals more confidence than any testimonial could.

5. Publish cost-per-gram

A simple comparison page — dose per serving, servings per container, cost per gram of actual NMN — turns the audience's own arithmetic habit into your acquisition channel. The premium brands lose this comparison by an order of magnitude and cannot respond without cutting price.

6. Be the stack coach, not the pill seller

TMG, timing, fat, storage, cycling, what to pair and what to skip. These are the highest-volume unanswered questions in the data. Owning them makes Prime the trusted operator of the protocol rather than one interchangeable bottle inside it — and it makes the bundle attach rate a natural consequence rather than an upsell.

7. Make the cold chain visible

Shipping heat is a live, unaddressed fear with real refund consequences. Whatever Prime actually does about stability — stabilised form, packaging, seasonal handling — say it explicitly and show it. If the product is heat-stable, that is a headline, not a footnote.

8. Recruit the stop-and-restart testimonial

Ask reviewers one question: "What happened when you ran out?" It produces the exact anecdote structure this market treats as proof, and it is far more persuasive than a five-star "love this product."

Three UGC scripts

A · The stop-and-restart

  1. Hook, 0–3s"I ran out of my NMN for nine days. That's how I know it works."
  2. Context, 3–10sAge, one specific baseline. "I'm 54. Before this, I was done by two in the afternoon."
  3. The gap, 10–20sWhat came back during the gap — named, physical, unglamorous. Stairs. The 3pm wall. Waking up foggy.
  4. The return, 20–28s"Four days back on it and it settled again."
  5. Product, 28–36sBottle in hand, batch number visible. "A full gram, and I can pull up the lab report on the label."
  6. CTA"Try it 60 days, then stop for ten. That's the test."

B · The certificate

  1. Hook, 0–3s"Most NMN on Amazon isn't NMN." Hold up a competitor-shaped generic bottle, unbranded.
  2. Stakes, 3–12sCite the third-party testing findings. Under 1% content. Some with zero.
  3. Turn, 12–22s"So here's the only thing I check now." Scan the batch code on Prime, show the COA loading on the phone screen.
  4. Read it, 22–32sPoint at three lines: identity, potency, heavy metals. Say the lab's name out loud.
  5. CTA"Don't take my word for it. Take the assay."

C · The niacin argument

  1. Hook, 0–4s"Every NMN video has the same comment: just take cheap niacin."
  2. Concede, 4–12sGive it real credit. Niacin raises NAD+ and it's cheap. Say so. Conceding buys the next ten seconds.
  3. Pivot, 12–24sThe flush. The megadose. The reason people quit it. Personal, specific, unpleasant.
  4. Position, 24–34s"So this is what I do instead. One gram, no flush, tested."
  5. CTA"If niacin works for you, genuinely — keep going. If you bounced off it, this is the other road."

Page and flow fixes

Product page — order matters

  1. Dose per serving, in milligrams, above the fold. Servings per container next to it.
  2. Cost per gram, calculated for them.
  3. Batch number + live COA link, not a badge.
  4. "Do I need TMG?" — answered on the page, not in a linked FAQ.
  5. Storage and shipping-heat answer, explicitly.
  6. Timeline expectation: what shows up when.
  7. The refund terms, stated in one sentence a skeptic can trust.

Post-purchase flow (Klaviyo)

  • Day 0 — How to take it: timing, with or without food, storage. Kills the top protocol anxieties before they become tickets.
  • Day 2 — The TMG question, answered honestly either way.
  • Day 7 — "You probably don't feel anything yet. Here's why that's expected." Single biggest churn-prevention email available.
  • Day 21 — What to watch for, in their language: morning ramp-up, the 3pm wall, day-after soreness.
  • Day 45 — Introduce the stop-and-restart test. Frame it as the brand's own confidence.
  • Day 60 — Review request with one question: "What changed, and what came back when you ran out?"

What to test first

If you only run three tests: certificate-led vs full-gram/dose-math-led vs stop-and-restart-led. Those three map to the three largest, most distinct clusters in the data, and they'll tell you which axis this brand should own before you spend on the rest.

Part 08

Guardrails

The most persuasive quotes in this corpus are unusable

The highest-emotion testimonials in the data describe reversing Alzheimer's, resolving lung scarring, curing arthritis, and recovering from cancer treatment. They are moving, they are specific, and putting any of them in an ad turns a supplement into an unapproved drug claim in the eyes of the FTC and FDA.

Keep to structure/function language: supports cellular energy, supports healthy NAD+ levels, supports healthy aging. Screen every piece of UGC for disease language before it goes live, including in comment replies and creator briefs — a claim made by your paid creator is your claim.

  • Substantiate testimonials. Keep the raw review, the date, and the customer's consent on file for anything you run as an ad. Add typicality language where results are unusual.
  • Don't run the conspiracy. The FDA-reactance sentiment is a real tailwind, but "Big Pharma is hiding this" in your own copy is a Meta rejection risk and a brand ceiling. Use availability framing instead.
  • Don't borrow Sinclair. Naming him is now roughly as likely to cost trust as to build it with this specific audience, and it invites the whole lobbying argument into your comments.
  • Only claim what your COA says. "99% purity" and "third-party tested" are powerful precisely because they're checkable — which means they must survive being checked, on every batch.
  • Health-claim review before scale. Have counsel review the evidence page and the responder-guarantee wording once. It's cheap now and expensive later.
