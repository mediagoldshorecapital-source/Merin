How to read this

This is deliberately broad before narrow. The nine lanes are the landscape, with real operators in each. The four directions narrow to what's available to us given what we already own. The six questions at the end are the only real blockers.

On the evidence

Most published creator-economy numbers come from platforms and agencies with something to sell, and they don't agree — one source puts the creator economy at $250B in 2026, another projects $480B, a third had it at $104B in 2024. Treat every figure here as directional, not decision-grade. The patterns across sources are far more reliable than the numbers, and where the patterns agree it's noted.

Section 01

Five forces shaping the market

Everything downstream depends on these. They are the consistent signal across every source.

01

Algorithms now favour humans over logos

Meta, TikTok, LinkedIn and YouTube have all shifted distribution toward real human accounts over brand accounts. Personal accounts pull roughly 2–3× the engagement of business accounts. This is the strongest argument for doing this at all — the brand handle is fighting the algorithm, a face isn't.

02

Trust moved from brands to identifiable people

Trust in anonymous brands has fallen while trust in named founders has risen. In supplements this is amplified — it's a category where nobody believes the packaging. A named human with a verifiable track record is now the cheapest trust available.

03

AI made content cheap, so proof became the product

62% of creators already use generative AI; 94% of brands working with creators have adopted AI tools. When everyone can generate a polished post, polish stops signalling anything. What's scarce is verifiable, first-hand proof — real numbers, real screenshots, real failures. The corollary: over-polish now reads as AI slop, and imperfection reads as human.

04

Over-niching is out; a point of view is in

The 2023-era "pick one hyper-specific niche forever" advice has softened. Wellness, business and building can coexist under one person if a coherent vision holds them together. But the audience still has to be specific even when the topics aren't — one audience, one promised result, several topics.

05

Monetization moved from reach to depth

Course-only creators saw a ~40% revenue decline since 2023 while community-based creators grew ~27% in 2025. Memberships are now the primary revenue foundation rather than one option among many, typically $26–$50/month. The winning stack is consistent everywhere: short-form for discovery → email for conversion → products and community for revenue. Ads and brand deals are the least durable layer.

Section 02

The landscape: nine working lanes

A catalog, not a ranking. Each lane is live in the market with real operators in it. Ratings are a judgement call, not survey data.

Lane · Protocol Documentarian

Here is my exact system, measured, published, free

Who: Bryan Johnson / Blueprint is the archetype — biological-age measurement, published protocols, a Netflix documentary that moved him mainstream, and a supplement line sold off the back of it. Blueprint extended into a female longevity protocol in 2026 with Kate Tolo as primary subject, which shows the archetype is replicable to a second person rather than locked to the founder.

Shape: Your own data, published relentlessly — measurement is the content. YouTube primary. The protocol is the funnel: the audience is pre-sold on the stack before the store loads. The moat is accumulated data you can't fake or shortcut.

Catch: You must actually live it. It's a lifestyle commitment, not a content calendar — and the highest-scrutiny lane once you're commercial.

Lane · Credentialed Specialist

I'm an MD, RD or PhD and I own one under-served problem

Who: Dr. Mary Claire Haver (The 'Pause Life) is the cleanest example — a physician who took perimenopause, a genuinely under-served problem, and built both an audience and a supplement line on it. Adjacent: Judy Greer's Wile, Rochelle Weitzner's Pause — the same midlife gap approached from celebrity and industry-executive credibility.

Why it's hot: A documented gap in US healthcare around perimenopause, where practitioners are under-educated and the downstream risk is real. Under-served + high-anxiety + recurring-purchase is the best possible combination for supplements.

Catch: Requires a credential you cannot fake. Without one this lane is only reachable through a co-founded brand with a named practitioner — a real and common structure, but a partnership decision rather than a content one.

Lane · Operator / Insider

I run a real business. Here's what actually happened this week

Who: Nik Sharma ("The DTC Guy", Sharma Brands, one of the most-read DTC newsletters, co-hosts Limited Supply with Moiz Ali of Native → P&G, $100M). And most relevantly: Ash Melwani of Obvi — a bootstrapped collagen brand, ~$30M in under three years, 250K+ customers, built substantially on community (a Facebook group they credit with a 5×) and creative volume (~40 new creatives a week). Catch: You need real numbers and the willingness to publish them. Sub-$1M isn't a disqualifier — it's the differentiator, because almost everyone teaching in this lane is either much bigger or making it up.

Lane · AI Operator / Builder-in-Public

Here's the agent stack running my actual company

Why it stands out: Two trends collide. AI has become baseline infrastructure, so the interesting question moved from "does this work" to "show me your system running a real P&L." And creators are now selling AI agents as a direct product — packaging a repeated pain point as an input-output promise, selling access to an agent trained on their methodology rather than access to their calendar. Newsletter-monetization trackers name "a paid AI agent" as one of the 2–3 models the strongest operators stack.

Shape: Build-in-public. "7 things I learned going from $0 to $X" threads are consistently the fastest growth driver. The founder-content rhythm is ~2 expertise posts, 1 building-in-public post, 1 story post per week. X and LinkedIn primary, YouTube for the long-form system flagship.

Catch: The lane is high in talk and nearly empty of proof — flooded with people describing agents, almost devoid of people running a real business on them in public. That gap is the whole opportunity, and credibility is binary: if the system isn't genuinely running things, it collapses on contact with a competent audience.

Section 03

What's dying

Consistent across every source. Several of these were best practice eighteen months ago.

Generic motivational content and follower-count chasing. The tactics that built brands in 2023–24 are actively counterproductive now.
Corporate polish. Plain language ("here's what I learned", "this mistake cost us") substantially outperforms buzzword-laden posts. Over-editing is out; natural pacing and visible imperfection are in.
Trend-chasing niches. Trending niches saturate within weeks. Picking one because it's trending is a burnout path.
Broad categories. "Fitness" is saturated; "kettlebell training for over-50s" has fewer competitors and higher conversion. Narrow the audience, not necessarily the topics.
Course-only businesses. Roughly 40% revenue decline since 2023. Courses are a component now, not a business.
One brief posted to four platforms. There is no universal algorithm — TikTok weights watch-completion and re-watch, Instagram weights saves and shares, LinkedIn actively suppresses content that looks like engagement bait.
Spreading across every platform at once. The consensus is one primary, one secondary. For consumer and supplement brands the recommended primary is YouTube; for operator positioning it's LinkedIn or X.

Section 07

Compliance guardrails

Non-negotiable in this category, and it binds the person, not just the brand.

Two regulators, two jobs. The FTC governs disclosure of material connections; the FDA governs what claims may be made. Both have to be satisfied.
Founders must disclose too. Posting about your own product on a personal account is a material connection requiring clear and conspicuous disclosure. Genuine enthusiasm is not an exemption.
Structure/function claims are allowed; disease claims are not. "Supports joint comfort" is a different legal object from "treats arthritis." Crossing that line draws warning letters to the brand and the individual.
Both parties are liable. The FTC states plainly that sponsoring brand and individual creator can each be held responsible for unsubstantiated claims or inadequate disclosure — and being paid increases their interest.
What does work in 2026 wellness content: founder-credibility content, ingredient-mechanism education, before/after against measurable metrics rather than disease outcomes, and citing peer-reviewed studies.

Practical implication: before any consumer-facing health content ships, we write a one-page claims policy and run every script through it. Under Direction C this constraint mostly disappears — another point in its favour.

Section 08

What I need from you

The only real blockers. Everything above holds regardless of the answers.

1

Who is the person?

Name, background, and what you're actually credible on. The whole thing hinges on this and I don't have it.

2

Camera or not?

Face-forward changes the platform ranking completely. Faceless is viable in 2026 but carries an AI-slop penalty unless there's a strong voice attached.

3

Realistic weekly hours?

Two to three hours and ten hours are different plans, not the same plan scaled.

4

How public with numbers?

Directions A and C are meaningfully weaker if we can't publish revenue and spend.

5

What's the actual goal?

Prime Ingredients revenue, or a second income stream? They point at different directions. C serves both, D serves the first, A serves the second.

6

Any credentialed partner available?

Only relevant if D interests you.

Section 09 · Conditional on C

The first ninety days

A sketch, not a commitment. The questions above come first. These phases are genuinely sequential — each one depends on the last.

Weeks 1–2

Foundation

Lock positioning and the one-sentence promise. Claim handles. Set up the newsletter. Pick the primary platform and commit for ninety days. Write the content-pillar definitions.

Weeks 3–4

Proof bank

Document the agent stack as it exists today — the newsletter agent end to end, the Canva → Klaviyo pipeline, the Triple Whale reporting loop. This is the flagship long-form asset; everything else gets clipped from it.

Weeks 5–8

Cadence

Three to five posts a week. The first ninety days are about voice and rhythm, not virality. Every post repurposes into a newsletter section. Start the running "what it cost / what it earned" series.

Weeks 9–12

First monetization test

Package one agent as a free template to build the list, then test a paid version. Open sponsor conversations once the newsletter has a real subscriber count.

Throughout

Build the agents on camera

Each new agent added to Prime Ingredients is an episode. The work and the content are the same activity — which is the entire point of this direction.
