Meta creative teardown · Prime Ingredients

Your losing ads are the ones people like most.

Six months of Meta creative, 1,220 ads, $381K of spend, analysed for what separates winners from losers. The finding that runs through everything: across every single month, the ads with the highest hook rate, hold rate and click-through rate produced the worst return. You are buying attention, and attention is not the same as intent.

Window 2026-02-08 → 2026-08-07 Source ads_table (Meta channel-reported) Ads analysed 1,220 Deep-dive set 68 ads over $700 spend
Read this first — three constraints on the analysis.

1. This uses Meta's own reported conversions, not Triple Whale attribution. Triple Whale's pixel produced no data before June, so it cannot cover a six-month window. Meta-reported ROAS runs below blended store ROAS by a varying factor (0.47× to 1.00× across months), so these numbers are valid for ranking ads against each other and invalid as an absolute profit bar. "Winner" below means top-tier relative performance, not "profitable."

2. The winner list is time-concentrated. Eleven of the twelve winners launched between February and April, when the whole account performed better. Account-level decay is therefore mixed into the winner/loser split. Every headline claim below is instead tested within each month, where that decay cancels out.

3. Ad longevity is an outcome, not a cause. Winners ran 54 days on average and losers 28, but that is because you turned the losers off. Longevity is excluded from the diagnostic patterns.
The one-paragraph version

Sorted into tiers, the pattern is perfectly monotonic and it runs the wrong way round: winners have the lowest click-through rate (5.29%) and losers the highest (7.18%) — but winners convert 4.26% of clicks against the losers' 2.61%. Losers buy 36% more clicks and convert them 39% worse, at a CPA of $58.09 versus $31.90. The single most predictive creative property in this account is not stopping power. It is qualification — how hard the ad works to filter out people who were never going to buy.

03 — Copy anatomy

Angles E and F say the same thing. One works.

These two openers describe an identical mechanism — NAD+ declines with age — and perform 53% apart on ROAS. The difference is entirely in construction, which makes this the cleanest natural experiment in the account.

Winner · Angle E · ROAS 1.41 · CPA $44.36
Tired of feeling tired as you age? It's not just aging... Your NAD+ levels are plummeting! Boost them with our NMN Complex and regain energy, focus & clarity! Discover how →
  • Opens on a question the reader answers "yes" to internally
  • Second person throughout — "your NAD+ levels", not "the molecule"
  • Committed verbs — plummeting, boost, regain
  • Benefit triad — energy, focus, clarity
  • Explicit CTA with directional cue
  • Short — ~220 characters
146 ads · $146,474 spend · 4 winners / 5 losers
Loser · Angle F · ROAS 0.92 · CPA $67.72
Feeling more tired as you age? It's not "just aging." Your NAD+ levels naturally drop — the molecule your cells need for clean, steady energy. Our NMN Complex helps support those levels so you can feel energized, focused…
  • Question buried in a paragraph, no line breaks
  • Explains the science before earning the attention
  • Hedged verbs — naturally drop, helps support
  • Benefits as prose, not a scannable list
  • No CTA in the visible portion
  • Runs long and gets truncated mid-benefit
436 ads · $54,582 spend · 0 winners / 9 losers
The operative difference is commitment. "Your NAD+ levels are plummeting" makes a claim the reader either accepts or rejects — it qualifies. "Your NAD+ levels naturally drop… our complex helps support those levels" is hedged into meaninglessness; nobody self-selects out, so you pay for clicks from people with no urgency. Hedging is the copy-level version of the same disease as the high-hook creative: it maximises audience and minimises intent.

06 — Replication

How to manufacture more of these

The winners are not stylistically diverse. They are three templates with interchangeable parts, which makes them straightforward to mass-produce and test.

  1. Template 1 — Direct symptom question (7 of 12 winners)

    Highest-volume proven structure. Swap the symptom and the benefit triad to generate variants.

    [Symptom question, second person] + "?" "It's not just [dismissive explanation]…" "Your [mechanism] is [committed verb: plummeting/collapsing]!" "[Strong verb] them with [product] and regain [benefit 1], [benefit 2] & [benefit 3]!" "[Explicit CTA] →"

    Variables to rotate: symptom (tired / drained / foggy / slow to recover / waking up unrested); dismissive explanation (just aging / just stress / just a busy season); benefit triad order; CTA verb.

  2. Template 2 — Category-quality contrast (3 of 12 winners, best CPM in the account)

    Currently used only for Astaxanthin. It has never been tried on the NMN flagship, which is 93.5% of revenue — that is the single biggest untested opportunity here.

    "Not all [category] is created equal." "Most cheap ones are [specific failure: synthetic, underdosed, unstable] — weak, ineffective, and a waste of money." "Meet [product] — sourced from [specific origin/spec]"

    Why it works: it disqualifies aggressively. A reader who does not already believe supplements vary in quality opts out at line one, so you stop paying for their click. It also posts the lowest CPM of any angle ($57.47 vs $98.37 for the volume workhorse).

  3. Template 3 — Warning + named mechanism (2 of 12 winners)

    Use sparingly. The broad "WARNING: you're not getting older, you're just running out of energy" variants sit at 1.31 ROAS; the winners are the ones that name a specific mechanism in the warning itself.

    "WARNING: [symptom] is not just [dismissive explanation]!" "Did you know your [named mechanism] [specific decline]?" "[Strong verb] them back up with [product] and feel [triad] again!"

  4. Kill the two losing angles outright

    Retire angle F (soft educational — 436 ads, zero winners, nine losers) and angle D (first-person story — ROAS 0.83, highest CPM). Between them they account for $61,675 of spend at an average ROAS of 0.91. Whatever replaces them cannot do worse.

  5. Drop the discount headline and re-test the field

    Remove "75% OFF TODAY ONLY" and run headline-free against a benefit-specific headline. The discount version lost on click quality in five months out of five, and you are already discounting 35% at the price level anyway.

  6. Change the metric you judge new creative on

    This is the structural fix. Stop promoting ads on hook rate, hold rate, CTR or thumb-stop — in this account all four are inversely related to return, and judging on them is how $61,980 went into the loser tier. Judge on conversions per click, and gate spend on it.

    Winner benchmark: conv/click ≥ 4.0% → scale Marginal: conv/click 3.0–4.0% → hold, iterate Kill: conv/click < 2.8% → turn off

    Give each new ad a fixed test budget of roughly 40 clicks before judging — at your $1.50 CPC that is about $60 — then apply the gate. Note that Triple Whale's own checkout event only fires on ~63% of purchases, so read this metric from Meta, not from the pixel, until that is fixed.

  7. Test the retention angle nobody has written yet

    97% of orders are now subscriptions and recurring charges are the only profitable order type in the business, yet not one ad in six months mentions subscription, replenishment, or "never run out." Every ad sells a first purchase. Given the economics — you lose $21.95 on acquisition and make $37.34 per re-bill — creative that pre-sells the subscription is the highest-leverage untested angle available.
