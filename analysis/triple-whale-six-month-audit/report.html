02 — Data integrity

Before you act on any of this, fix the instrumentation

Eleven separate problems make parts of the warehouse unusable. The first three are severe enough that any decision made from a Triple Whale dashboard between January and May was made on wrong numbers.

Critical

A 27-day hole in the order data — April 6 to May 2

Orders effectively stop on 6 April and resume on 3 May. Eleven orders are recorded across the entire window. Meanwhile Meta kept spending roughly $2,100 a day and — critically — Meta's own pixel kept reporting 25 to 67 conversions a day worth $2,000–$4,100. Purchases were happening. Triple Whale simply did not ingest them.

This is a Shopify → Triple Whale sync failure, not a business collapse. It means April's 0.28 ROAS is fiction, and roughly $90–100K of revenue is missing from the warehouse. Ask Triple Whale support to backfill the range; until they do, exclude April from every comparison.

Gap spend $55,799 · orders recorded 11 · Meta-reported April conversions 1,383 worth $88,269 · warehouse April revenue $17,091

Critical

No cost of goods before May — early profit is overstated by roughly $46K

COGS coverage is exactly 0% for January through April, then jumps to 94–97% from May onward. Every profit, margin, and POAS figure Triple Whale showed you for the first four months excluded the single largest variable cost.

Applying July's actual 30.8% COGS ratio: February's reported +$21,293 net profit was really about +$5,800, and March's reported +$35,637 was really about +$4,500. The business was thinner than it looked from day one.

Orders with COGS — Jan 0/313 · Feb 0/839 · Mar 0/1,625 · Apr 8/264 · May 1,424/1,518 · Jul 2,107/2,216

Critical

No attribution data at all before June — $230,883 of spend flew blind

The Triple Whale pixel produced zero sessions until May and only became meaningful in June (23,113 sessions), then July (46,045). Every dollar spent from January through May was allocated with no channel, campaign, or creative attribution whatsoever.

Pixel sessions by month — Jan–Apr 0 · May 761 · Jun 23,113 · Jul 46,045 · Aug 1–7 6,978 · blind spend Jan–May $230,883

Warning

Shipping cost is set equal to shipping revenue — real cost is unknown

From January through May, shipping_costs matches shipping_price to the cent every single month. That is a default, not a measurement: it assumes you ship for exactly what customers pay you. Handling fees are $0 across all eight months, which is also not credible for a physical supplement.

Both understate true costs, so the contribution figures in this report are, if anything, generous.

Feb $3,477.08 = $3,477.08 · Mar $6,101.32 = $6,101.32 · Apr $901.49 = $901.49 · May $5,806.43 = $5,806.43 · handling fees $0 in every month

Warning

Checkout tracking is broken — July recorded more orders than checkouts

July shows 1,491 checkout events against 2,364 attributed orders. An order cannot exist without a checkout, so the checkout event is firing on roughly 63% of purchases. Any funnel drop-off analysis built on this is wrong, and the add-to-cart rate is likely undercounted too.

Jun 646 checkouts / 891 orders · Jul 1,491 / 2,364 · Aug 1–7 180 / 512

Warning

Klaviyo is connected but sends no data

Sent, delivered, opened, clicked, subscribed and unsubscribed are all null for every month. Only order attribution comes through, and it is tiny: $1,028 in July against $128,676 of revenue. You cannot see list growth, deliverability, or flow performance at all.

Klaviyo-attributed revenue — Jun $86 · Jul $1,028 · Aug 1–7 $244 · all engagement metrics null

Cleanup

Four smaller data defects

Inventory is corrupt — 1,312,982,987 items valued at $88.2 billion, with 240 items missing cost. Product COGS is incomplete — three of your four products have null cost, so only the flagship contributes to margin math. Sixteen orders carry a 100% "Custom discount", giving away $1,379 at zero revenue. Ad naming is inconsistent — - Video - Ad -2, - Video - Ad - 2 and Video - Ad - 2 are three different rows for what looks like related creative, which quietly fragments every creative report.

03 — Unit economics

The number to run the business on is 1.70

After cost of goods, payment fees, shipping and refunds, roughly 59 cents of every revenue dollar is left to pay for advertising. That sets a hard breakeven blended ROAS of about 1.70 — and it is the single most useful number in this report.

04 — The engine

The subscription pivot is the only thing working

In May, zero orders were subscriptions. By July, 97% were. This is the most consequential change in the entire dataset and it is barely visible in the standard dashboards.

You are discounting your own recurring revenue by 38%. In June, recurring charges carried a 5.1% discount. By July that had jumped to 35.4% and in August 37.9% — the same discount new customers get. Recurring charges need no acquisition incentive; the customer has already chosen to stay. On July's recurring volume alone, cutting that to 20% is worth roughly $6,700 a month, and it scales with the base.
This also explains the strange June cohort. Customers who first bought in June show a 55.5% repeat rate against 5–16% for every other cohort. That is not a fluke of behaviour — it is the subscription program launching in June and re-billing those customers in July. Cohort repeat rates before June are the true organic repeat rate, and they are low: 4.8% to 6.1%.

07 — Before further action

What I would do, in this order

Ordered by return on effort. The first three cost nothing but a support ticket and a settings change.

  1. Stop discounting recurring charges

    Recurring subscribers already chose to stay; the 38% markdown buys nothing. Take it to 15–20% — still a real subscriber benefit — and the margin lands straight on the bottom line.

    ≈ +$6,700/month at July volume, scaling with the recurring base · no traffic change required

  2. Kill campaigns 8 and 11

    ROAS 0.98 and 0.73, new-customer CAC 45–60% above campaign 9. Move the budget into campaign 9, which is the cheapest genuine acquisition source you have. Do not move it into campaign 4 — its ROAS is inflated by returning customers.

    $20,727 reallocated over 60 days · ≈ 90 more new customers at campaign 9's CAC

  3. Open a Triple Whale ticket for the April 6 – May 2 backfill

    Give them the exact window and the evidence: Meta reported 1,383 April conversions worth $88,269 against 264 recorded orders. Until it is backfilled, exclude April from every report and comparison.

    Recovers ~$90–100K of missing revenue history · unblocks all year-over-year and trend analysis

  4. Fix the cost settings

    Add COGS for the three products that have none, replace the shipping-cost default with real carrier rates, and enter handling fees. Then backfill COGS to January so the first four months become comparable.

    Makes contribution profit trustworthy · current figures are optimistic by an unknown margin

  5. Repair the checkout event and the Klaviyo feed

    The checkout event fires on roughly 63% of purchases, so funnel diagnosis is impossible. Klaviyo sends no engagement data at all, and is attributing $1,028 a month against a 5,000-customer base — email is your cheapest route to the recurring charges the model depends on, and right now you cannot even see it.

    Unblocks funnel optimisation and the single cheapest retention lever available

  6. Instrument subscription churn before scaling spend again

    The entire model rests on 0.59 recurring charges per new subscriber. You have two months of data and it is clearing the bar by a narrow margin. Track cycle-2 and cycle-3 retention weekly — if first-cycle retention drops below 60%, scaling acquisition makes losses worse, not better.

    Determines whether August's profitability is a floor or a one-off

  7. Start reducing single-channel and single-product exposure

    100% of spend on Meta and 93.5% of revenue from one SKU means a single ad-account issue or supply problem takes the business to zero. This is not urgent this week, but it should be on the quarter's plan.
